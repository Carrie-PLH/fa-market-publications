# Lobster Monitor

**Issue 1: October 2026**
Field Assembly Market Publications · October 18, 2026 · Data run `2026-10-18T144903Z`

Issue 0 set the baseline. This issue is the first read against it. It is written for anyone facing a supplier's lobster price claim: an event client with an escalation clause, a planner comparing quotes, a small buyer asked to accept an increase. It reports market evidence. It does not estimate any supplier's cost and draws no conclusion about any contract.

## The read

The gap between lobster and seafood has widened, and it is now wide enough that the two numbers tell opposite stories. The producer price index for lobsters is 1.3% below September 2025. The producer price index for all unprocessed and prepared seafood is 20.1% above it. Over two years the lobster index is down 2.4% and the seafood index is up 31.3%. A supplier who says seafood costs are up a fifth on the year is citing a real published series. It is not a lobster series, and the lobster series went the other way.

Dock prices and landings tell the longer story, and that story has not changed. Maine landings fell from 132.6 million pounds in 2016 to a preliminary 78.8 million in 2025, down 40.6%. The annual ex-vessel price rose from $4.08 a pound to $5.85 over the same period. A supplier pointing at a decade of tightening supply is on firm ground. A supplier pointing at it to explain a change since last season is not: the September dock price was 2.1% lower in 2025 than in 2024.

Imports through Portland are the one place something moved sharply. August volume was 329,208 kg against 924,011 kg a year earlier, down 64.4%, and the January-to-August total is 14.9% below the same months of 2025, 13.9 million kg against 16.4 million. The price of those imports did not follow: unit value was $9.02 a pound against $8.99, up 0.3%. Less lobster came through at about the same price.

## The explanation

Three prices describe the same lobster at different points in the chain. They move together over a decade and separately over a year.

The ex-vessel price is what the boat gets at the dock, and Maine's most recent September figure is $6.15 a pound, from 2025 and marked preliminary. Import unit value is what live Canadian lobster costs entering through Portland, $9.02 a pound in August 2026. At the counter, a single 1.25 lb hard shell ran $18.90 to $19.50 at two Maine sellers, which is $15.12 to $15.60 a pound at the stated minimum weight. The distance between the dock and the counter is handling, grading, mortality and margin, and it is where most of a buyer's price sits.

Seasonality still governs the lobster index more than the year does. Prices peak in late winter, when landings are lowest and the catch is hard shell, and fall through summer as the new-shell catch lands. The 2026 winter peak ran above 2025's, 334 in March against 315, a difference of 5.9%. By September both years had fallen back to within a point and a half of each other. A quote priced in March and an adjustment claimed in October compare two seasons, not two years.

What the import figures cannot settle is why the volume fell. Lower Canadian volume through one district can mean less supply, weaker demand, or lobster routed through another port, and this record cannot distinguish them. Maine's own 2026 landings will not be published until early 2027. USDA's Food Price Outlook puts fish and seafood 6.5% higher from August 2025 to August 2026 and gives a 2027 forecast interval of −3.8% to 6.6%. USDA does not forecast lobster separately.

## The uncertainty

This issue compares each series to the same period a year earlier and, where the record holds it, two years earlier. Month-to-month movement is not in this issue's computed set, so nothing here measures September against August.

The dock price is a year old. Maine DMR publishes monthly landings once a year, in February, and marks the most recent year preliminary. No public 2026 ex-vessel series exists. Any claim about 2026 dock prices in Maine rests on private data.

August import figures are recent and subject to revision. NOAA trade data for recent months is routinely restated, and a 64.4% volume fall in a single month at a single district is the kind of figure that moves on revision. The year-to-date fall of 14.9% is the steadier number.

The producer price index for lobsters is an index, not a price. Its composition is not published in enough detail to say which products or channels drive it.

The consumer reference is thin, and thinner this month than last. It rests on two sellers, D and G, because only two of the eight sellers observed list a single live hard-shell 1.25 lb lobster. Seller G was observed on both capture dates at $19.50 and did not move. Seller D was observed on October 15 only, at $18.90. Across all eight sellers, 39 live-lobster listings were observed on both dates and none changed price. That count is well below last month's because four sellers were lost at capture on October 1 to one HTTP 403 and three HTTP 429 responses; their listings are marked unavailable for that date in the record rather than omitted, and the failures are recorded in the observation record's flags. A fifth seller served a bot-check page instead of its product list and is recorded as a parse failure for that date.

The chain comparison mixes dates. The dock price is September 2025, the import value is August 2026, and the counter price is October 2026. The spread between them is illustrative and is not a margin calculation.

## The buyer's question

Ask the supplier three things before accepting a lobster-driven increase.

Which product: live, meat or tail; what size and shell; what grade. These move differently, and a quote for 1.25 lb hard shell cannot be checked against a claim about meat.

Which dates: the date the original quote priced from, and the date the adjustment references. A March-to-October comparison is a season, not a market move.

Which series: the published series or supplier invoice behind the stated increase. If the answer is that seafood prices are up, ask whether the series quoted is the seafood index or the lobster index. Both are free and public, and this month they point in opposite directions.
