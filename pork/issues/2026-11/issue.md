# Pork Monitor

**Issue 2: November 2026**
Field Assembly Market Publications · Information as of November 16, 2026 · Data run `2026-11-16T134201Z`

This is the second issue of the Pork Monitor. It is written for a restaurant, barbecue business or caterer facing a supplier's price claim on pork. It reports market evidence. It does not estimate any supplier's cost and draws no conclusion about any contract.

## The read

Wholesale pork values are further below a year ago than they were in the October issue. The pork carcass cutout, USDA's estimated value of a standard hog carcass at the packing plant, was $71.66 per hundredweight in the week ending November 6, against $100.58 in the comparable week of 2025, a fall of 28.8%. Its four-week average is 26.1% lower. All six primal values are lower on the year, by very different amounts: the belly 48.3%, the picnic 35.1% and the ham 32.0%, against the loin 16.7%, the rib 16.3% and the butt 14.5%. A claim framed as "pork" may rest on any one of these, and which one matters.

Set beside the October issue, which read the week ending October 9, the single-week fall on the year is larger for the carcass cutout and for every one of the six primals. On the four-week averages the fall is larger for the carcass cutout and for four of the primals, and smaller for the belly and the butt.

None of these is the price of a cut a kitchen orders. The cutout and the primals are estimated values per hundredweight, FOB plant, for a 215-pound carcass from barrows and gilts, across all sales types. A belly value is not a bacon price and a loin value is not a price for chops. The explanation below gives the specification.

The producer price indexes point the same way. The index for slaughter hogs is 23.4% below October 2025, and the index for fresh and frozen pork made in slaughtering plants is 32.9% below. Both falls are larger than the September ones the October issue reported.

Retail has no year-earlier comparison this month. The Bureau of Labor Statistics published no October 2025 value for the two consumer price indexes or the two average retail prices this title carries: the series file retained in this issue's run marks that month "Data unavailable due to the 2025 lapse in appropriations" for all four of the consumer series this title carries. Four comparisons therefore do not exist in this issue. The average retail price of all pork chops was $4.176 a pound in October and of sliced bacon $6.686 a pound, and this record cannot say whether either is higher or lower than in October 2025. The same is true of the consumer price index for pork and the consumer price index for food away from home. The producer price indexes were published for October 2025, which is why their comparisons stand.

What the record does hold for those four is a comparison with October 2024. Against that month pork chops are 2.3% lower, sliced bacon is 2.7% lower, the pork consumer price index is 1.0% higher and the index for food away from home is 7.6% higher. A two-year comparison does not stand in for the missing one-year comparison: it does not show in which of the two years the movement came. The latest year-over-year reading of pork consumer prices this record holds is September's, from USDA's Food Price Outlook: 0.7% lower from September 2025 to September 2026.

Wholesale and retail figures are different measures and not one price read at different points in the chain, so a difference between how far each has moved is not a margin, and this record cannot say what it is made of. What these figures establish is narrow. None of the November wholesale values and neither October producer price index is higher than a year earlier, so they do not support a general claim that wholesale pork values rose on the year. On October retail prices against a year earlier, the record is silent. None of this establishes what happened to any particular supplier's costs.

Comparison dates matter. The slaughter hog index is 23.4% below October 2025 and 6.6% below October 2024; in the October issue it stood above its level of two years earlier, and it no longer does. The producer index for fresh and frozen pork is 26.0% below October 2024. And within the last four weeks the primals did not move together: the carcass cutout fell every week, from $80.26 to $71.66 per hundredweight, and the belly from $90.06 to $75.45, while the ham fell from $68.42 to $61.11 and then rose to $63.00 in the latest week, the only primal that rose in that week.

The hog inventory figure has not changed since the October issue, because USDA has published no new estimate. Its survey estimate of all hogs and pigs in the United States on September 1, 2026 was 74,302 thousand head, 1.5% fewer than a year earlier, with the breeding herd 1.0% smaller and market hogs 1.5% fewer. These are counts of animals, not prices. The report's own reliability statement shows that over the past 20 quarters its first estimate of this total has differed from its latest by more than this year's change at least once, and a head count on one date is not a measure of how much pork is produced. A smaller inventory alongside lower wholesale values is what the record shows; it holds no measure of demand, exports or cold storage, and it cannot say what drove the price.

## The explanation

Five kinds of measure appear above and they answer different questions. The cutout and primal values are estimates USDA computes each week from reported sales of pork cuts at the plant. The producer price indexes measure the selling prices received by domestic producers and exclude imports: one for live hogs sold for slaughter, one for fresh and frozen pork made in slaughtering plants. The two consumer price indexes measure the change in prices urban consumers pay: one for pork at retail, one for meals and snacks bought away from home, which is not a restaurant's cost. The two average prices are retail prices per pound for two items. The NASS figures are counts of animals, in head, not prices at all. None of them is a supplier's cost, and a movement in one does not fix a movement in another.

The weekly series are narrower than the word "pork" suggests, and none of them is the price of a cut a buyer orders. Report 2680 states the cutout as the estimated value of a standard 215-pound carcass, fifty-five to fifty-six percent lean with 0.55 to 0.70 inches of backfat at the last rib, from barrows and gilts only, in dollars per hundred pounds, FOB plant: priced at the packing plant, so freight to the buyer is not included. It is built from the prices and volume of pork cuts delivered and from average industry cutting yields, over one week of trade, Monday to Friday, across all destinations including export, and all sales types, delivery periods, refrigeration types and packaging styles, except specialty pork products. Whole-carcass trades in the week, if there are any, are averaged in at one to one. It is not a price paid for hogs and it does not cover sow or boar pork. It is a computed value for a whole carcass, not a price offered to any kitchen.

The six primal values are estimated values for parts of that same carcass, on the same basis, built from the individual cuts and their prices. They are not the price of any single cut: a belly value is not a bacon price, and a loin value is not a price for chops. The report does not list which cuts make up each primal or give their cutting yields, and it does not say whether a primal value is per hundred pounds of that primal or of carcass. A primal value is best read for its level against other weeks and its change on the year, not converted into a cost per pound of anything a kitchen receives.

The belly is the largest move on the year, on both comparisons, and the two differ because of where each year's latest week sits against its own four weeks. A year ago the belly stood at $145.99 per hundredweight in the comparable week, above its four-week average of $141.53. This year the latest week, $75.45, is below its four-week average of $83.55. So the single-week fall, 48.3%, is larger than the four-week fall, 41.0%. The ham runs the other way: its latest week, $63.00, is the one week of the four in which it rose, and a year ago its comparable week, $92.64, was below its four-week average of $98.52, so its four-week fall, 35.0%, is larger than its single-week fall, 32.0%. The carcass cutout itself fell week by week across the four, from $80.26 to $77.15, $73.64 and $71.66. The rib holds the highest value of the six, $132.36, and the picnic the lowest, $51.90.

The six primals fall into two groups this month, and the grouping holds on either comparison; inside the second group the ham and the picnic change places. On the latest week the butt, the rib and the loin are lower by between 14.5% and 16.7%, and the ham, the picnic and the belly by between 32.0% and 48.3%. On the four-week averages the butt, the rib and the loin are lower by between 10.6% and 15.0%, and the picnic, the ham and the belly by between 30.9% and 41.0%. A claim resting on the first group and a claim resting on the second are being measured against falls of very different size.

Retail cannot be set beside wholesale on a year's footing this month. The belly primal is 48.3% lower and the loin primal 16.7% lower, the week ending November 6 against November 7, 2025; sliced bacon and pork chops at retail have no October 2025 value to compare with. Against October 2024 sliced bacon is 2.7% lower and pork chops 2.3% lower, and the computed set holds no two-year comparison for the primals to put next to those. The record does not say which primal either retail item is cut from. These figures show what each series did, not how a primal value passes into a retail price.

USDA's Food Price Outlook, in its update of October 23, 2026, puts pork consumer prices 0.7% lower from September 2025 to September 2026, meats 1.3% higher and food away from home 3.5% higher. It gives forecast intervals for 2027 of −8.0% to 8.9% for pork, −8.7% to 10.0% for meats, and 0.7% to 5.0% for food away from home. The pork interval is wide and runs from a fall to a rise.

## The uncertainty

This is the second issue of this title. It compares each series with the same period a year earlier and, where the record holds it, two years earlier. Where it compares with the October issue it does so in words, saying whether a fall on the year is larger or smaller than the one that issue reported, or which side of an earlier level an index stood, because the October issue's figures are not in this issue's computed set. Month-to-month movement is not in the computed set either, so nothing here measures October against September.

Four year-earlier comparisons do not exist: the pork consumer price index, the consumer price index for food away from home, the average retail price of sliced bacon and the average retail price of all pork chops. BLS published no October 2025 value for any of them and marks the month "Data unavailable due to the 2025 lapse in appropriations." The gap is in the source, not in this record's retrieval, and a later run will not fill it unless BLS publishes those values. The two producer price indexes carry October 2025 values and are compared in the ordinary way.

The cutout and primals run a week behind this month's beef cutout. Report 2680 normally publishes on Monday for the week ending the previous Friday, later than the Beef Monitor's weekly cutout report. At this issue's run, on Monday, November 16, the newest week in the source was the week ending November 6, published November 9; the week ending November 13 was not yet in it. The Beef Monitor's November computed set carries the boxed beef cutout to the week ending November 13, so a reader setting this cutout beside it is comparing different weeks. Cheddar Check's November computed set carries its CME series to the week ending November 6, the same week as this cutout; in October they were a week ahead of it. Those statements rest on those titles' own evidence files, not on a capture listed in this issue's.

The pork cutout is a comprehensive value: one weekly value across all sales types and all delivery periods. The boxed beef cutout, as the Beef Monitor's methodology describes it, is Choice and Select graded, from negotiated sales delivered within 0 to 21 days. They are not the same kind of measure, and a difference between them is partly a difference in what each counts.

A year-over-year comparison for the weekly series is against the week ending nearest 52 weeks earlier, within four days; here the week ending November 6, 2026 is compared with the week ending November 7, 2025. The four-week figures are simple averages of four weekly values, the weeks ending October 16, October 23, October 30 and November 6, each week counting equally, against the four weeks ending October 17 to November 7, 2025. Weighting those weeks by the volume traded behind them could give a different number. Each row is labeled for the method used.

The year-over-year falls in this issue are large. Each weekly value, its year-earlier week, and the four weekly values behind each average in both years were recalculated from the retained report 2680 file before publication, and each matched the computed set. That confirms the arithmetic and the reading of the source. It does not explain the falls, and this record holds nothing that would.

Mandatory price reporting covers trade at reporting plants. It is not every transaction in the country.

The October producer price indexes are preliminary, and BLS may revise them for up to four months after first publication. The series file retained in this run still marks the July, August and September indexes preliminary, and its August and September readings for fresh and frozen pork are not the ones in the file retained for the October issue (run `2026-10-18T145155Z`, listed in that issue's `evidence.json`). September values are not in this issue's computed set and are not restated here.

The hog inventory is quarterly and has not been updated since the October issue. The September 1 estimate was published on September 24, and it is the newest supply figure this record holds, a count now more than ten weeks old; the next estimate is for December 1, and the ESMIS publication page retained in this run lists the next release for December 23, 2026. NASS may revise its estimates for the previous four quarters at each release, so the September 1, 2025 figure here is the one the September 2026 release prints and may differ from what was first published. The record holds no September 2024 reading, so the inventory carries no two-year comparison. The report gives weight groups in pounds and does not say whether they are live weights. The pig crop and farrowing figures the same report publishes are not parsed into this record.

The two retail average prices are U.S. city averages for all pork chops and for sliced bacon. They are what shoppers paid at retail, not a foodservice price and not a price for a particular cut, thickness or pack.

The USDA Food Price Outlook figures are year-over-year from September 2025 to September 2026, a month earlier than this issue's October indexes. The forecast intervals are for 2027. They cover pork, meats and food away from home as categories.

The cutout and primal values are USDA estimates built from reported sales, not an offer available to any particular buyer, and a restaurant's delivered cost includes processing, freight and terms this record does not see.

## The buyer's question

Ask the supplier three things before accepting a pork increase.

Which product and which basis: the cut, how it is trimmed and packed, whether it is fresh or frozen, and which primal the supplier says it comes from. The weekly figures above are estimated primal values FOB plant on a barrow and gilt carcass, not the price of a case of any product, and in the latest week the six primals were lower on the year by amounts ranging from 14.5% to 48.3%.

Which dates: the date the original quote priced from, and the date the adjustment references. The carcass cutout went from $80.26 to $71.66 per hundredweight across four weeks; a quote priced from an earlier week can sit some way from the latest one.

Which series: the published series or supplier invoice behind the stated increase. Every series above is free and public. In the latest period each carries, the cutout, all six primals and both producer price indexes are lower than a year earlier. For the pork consumer price index and both retail average prices, an October comparison with a year earlier does not exist in the BLS series, and a claim that cites one should be asked for its source.
